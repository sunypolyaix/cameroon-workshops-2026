Where we start

Every tool carries a politics.

To use a tool is to be entangled in who made it, who benefits, and who pays. AI is not an exception to the history of tools. It is a new case.

The spine of the talk

Understanding grounds accountability.

Ethical research begins by understanding the tool — what it is, how it was made, why it was made, and who governs its use.

How tools are made

Three material realities behind every prompt.

People

  • Research and engineering
  • Annotation and evaluation
  • Content moderation
  • Maintenance and support

Resources

  • Data: collected, licensed, inferred, or appropriated
  • Electricity and water
  • Chips, minerals, data centers, waste

Institutions

  • Capital investment
  • Governance and contracts
  • Law, standards, and procurement
  • Organizations that control access

Labor, data, energy, capital, and governance are not background conditions. They are part of the system.

Why tools are made

Motives overlap. That is the ethical problem.

AI systems are made for knowledge and public benefit, but also for profit, coordination, competition, prestige, and control. Research ethics requires seeing all of those motives at once.

Access is not permission

Four questions researchers must not collapse.

Technical, legal, relational, contextual

  • Accessibility asks: Can I get the material?
  • Permission asks: Do I have the legal right to use it?
  • Consent asks: Did the affected people meaningfully agree?
  • Legitimacy asks: Is this use appropriate here?

Cameroon matters

Cameroon follows droit d'auteur, with statutory copyright exceptions rather than a U.S.-style open-ended fair-use test. If your intended use is not licensed or covered by an exception, technical accessibility does not create permission.

Reference points: Law No. 2000/011 of 19 Dec. 2000 on Copyright and Neighbouring Rights; WIPO Lex; OAPI/Bangui Agreement; Berne; TRIPS.

Movement Two

Making Research Accountable

Not commandments. Practices that make judgment inspectable.

Practice 1

Document what you did.

Prompting, coding, retrieval, editing, comparison, and synthesis are different activities. Name the activity. Preserve the workflow.

Minimum record

  • Prompts and major iterations
  • Models, tools, harnesses, and interfaces used
  • What was ingested for RAG or analysis
  • Where human judgment changed the output
  • What the model described vs. what you verified

Citation is provenance infrastructure

  • Prefer DOI, ISBN, URI, and stable identifiers over bare URLs
  • Maintain full BibTeX metadata
  • Keep Markdown source, not only PDF output
  • If transforming PDFs, preserve conversion provenance
  • Open cited sources; verify the quotation and the claim
Practice 2

Cite so another person can check you.

A citation is not decorative. It is a path back to evidence. AI raises the standard because fabrication is easy and often plausible.

Practice 3

Disclose the process, not just the tool.

"I used ChatGPT" is rarely enough. Ethical disclosure explains how AI shaped the research process and where responsibility remained with the researcher.

A disclosure note should say

  • What tools were used
  • For which activities
  • What data or texts were provided
  • How outputs were checked
  • What remains the author's responsibility

Generate one disclosure.md file for each paper or project.

Reproducibility is generosity

  • Keep the full conversation history when possible
  • Store artifacts in a structured workbench
  • Use Markdown, BibTeX, and stable files
  • Share enough for another person or model to reproduce, critique, or improve the work
Practice 4

Share so the work can be improved.

The goal is not merely to defend yourself. The goal is to make research legible enough that others can evaluate and extend it.

Movement Three

Responsibility and Judgment

Ethical research is not only what you can defend to yourself. It is what you can defend across contexts.

Choices and tradeoffs

Every AI decision has four audiences.

  • Personal: what cognition do you offload, and why?
  • Institutional: what policies govern your research and data?
  • Professional: what do your disciplinary codes require?
  • Social: who bears costs, who gains capacity, and who is excluded?
The obligation that follows

Early adopters set the norms.

It does not matter only how you got here — merit, geography, timing, family, institution. What matters is that your fluency will shape what others think responsible AI research looks like.

Someone teaches first. If you are among the first, that someone is you.

Direction, not abstinence

Energy is never free.

The question is not whether research spends energy, labor, attention, or institutional trust. Research always spends something.

The question is whether what stands at the end was worth the cost — and whether you can defend that judgment publicly.

Point the compute somewhere worth the joules.

Before you go, answer three questions.

  • Where are you, honestly, on the awareness spectrum?
  • Which part of your AI process is least documented?
  • Who is one person you could teach this framework to in the next month?

Research ethics begins with understanding. It becomes real through accountable practice.
