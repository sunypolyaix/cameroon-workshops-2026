Where we start

Every tool carries a politics.

To use a tool is to be entangled in who made it, who benefits, and who pays. That question is old. You are just meeting it freshly.

Your students are engineers and scientists studying water flows, grids, soils — not AI itself. That doesn't exempt them from this question. It puts them at the front of it.

Movement Two

Operational Principles —
Friction Points, Not Commandments

Every one of these is contestable. That's the point — it teaches judgment.

Cluster A

Transparency

Disclosure

Be upfront about where and how you used AI in your work.

Capture your process

Keep a record of prompts, iterations, and decisions — not just the polished output.

Honesty about computation

Be precise: what did the tool compute or generate, and what did you determine or verify?

Cluster B

Tool Literacy

Understand your tools

Know their biases and their provenance — what data trained them, whose choices shaped them.

Verify

Check AI-generated claims — especially citations — against primary sources. Fabrication is real and well documented.

Cluster C

Stewardship & Boundaries

Data stewardship

Don't feed confidential or personally identifying data into tools that may retain or reuse it.

Consent Friction point

Private / personally-identifying data needs consent. Published, publicly available material generally falls under fair use for scholarly analysis — no consent required. Reasonable people place that line differently.

Security & acceptable use

On university systems, you're bound by their policies — and you own the risk of what you expose.

Licensing

Know what you're permitted to use as input, and how your own output is licensed downstream.

Cluster D

Ownership

Reproducibility

Could someone else retrace your steps and land in the same place?

Accountability

You own the output — in full — regardless of what tool produced it.

Not a checklist

Contested terrain, not eleven rules.
