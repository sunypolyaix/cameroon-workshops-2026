# Research Ethics for AI-Assisted Graduate Research

**Session 4 · University of Buea · SUNY Poly AIX · Monday, July 20, 2026**

<p><a href="slides.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Open the slide deck ↗</a> · <a href="../">Back to Session 4</a></p>

This is not a checklist. It is a way of reasoning, one you'll keep using long after this
workshop ends. Everything below is contested terrain — friction points to navigate, not
commandments to obey. The one idea that doesn't bend: **the material grounds the
procedural.** Understand what a tool actually costs before you argue about how to use it
correctly.

---

## 1. Material awareness — the first obligation

Every tool carries a politics. The loom, the printing press, the smartphone — to use one
is to be entangled in who made it, who benefits, and who pays. Large language models are
the newest entry in that lineage, not an exception to it.

Before anything procedural, know what you're holding:

| Impact | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Human labor** | Behind every model is unpaid or underpaid human work — data labeling, content moderation, annotation. The fluency you're gaining rests on labor you'll likely never see. |
| **Environmental cost** | Training and running these models consumes real water and real energy. A single query is small; billions of queries are not. |
| **Equity** | Access to these tools is not evenly distributed — globally, nationally, or in this room. Who benefits, and who is left further behind? |

This maps onto the classic sustainability frame — people, planet, fairness — applied to AI
specifically.

### How aware do you need to be?

Awareness is a spectrum, not a box you check. Locate yourself honestly:

- **Floor** — you've read the equivalent of three Wikipedia articles: what an LLM is, how
  it's trained, what a data center costs to run.
- **Ceiling** — you've read Kate Crawford's *Atlas of AI* (or a comparable full scholarly
  treatment of labor, environment, or data extraction).
- **Somewhere between the two** is a reasonable place for a working researcher to stand —
  as long as you know where you're standing, and you keep moving up the ladder over time.

### Where you stand: the Cameroon context

You are not an anonymous global user. As of early 2025, roughly **42% of Cameroonians**
use the internet at all, and Cameroon sits among the bottom ten African countries for
mobile broadband — about **30 subscriptions per 100 people**, against a continental
average of **52** (ITU / DataReportal, 2025). A graduate researcher online right now, with
a functioning connection, is sitting comfortably in the **top tenth of the country**.

That position was not necessarily earned by merit alone — and it doesn't matter how you
got here. What matters is this: your government, your university, and this workshop's
institutions have made a real investment in getting you this fluency. That investment
carries an implicit return: **not to hoard the capability, but to spread it** — to
colleagues, students, and communities still outside the frame. Early adopters set the
norms everyone else inherits. In the diffusion of any technology, someone has to go first
and then teach.

The ethics here is about *direction*, not *abstinence*. You don't need to feel guilty
about the compute you use — you need to point it somewhere worth the joules: toward
research that improves someone's situation, toward capability you'll pass on to others.

## 2. Operational principles — friction points, not commandments

These are real obligations, but every one of them is contestable. The point of naming them
as friction points is to teach judgment, not rule-following.

### Transparency
- **Disclosure** — be upfront about where and how you used AI in your work.
- **Capture your process** — keep a record of prompts, iterations, and decisions, not just
  the final output.
- **Honesty about computation** — be precise about what the tool computed or generated
  versus what you determined, verified, or decided yourself.

### Tool literacy
- **Understand your tools** — know their biases and their provenance (what data trained
  them, whose choices shaped them).
- **Verify** — check AI-generated claims, and especially citations, against primary
  sources. Fabrication is real and well-documented.

### Stewardship & boundaries
- **Data stewardship** — don't feed confidential, sensitive, or personally identifying data
  into tools that may retain or reuse it.
- **Consent — a genuine friction point.** Private or personally-identifying data requires
  consent from the people involved. Published or publicly available material generally
  falls under fair use for scholarly analysis — no consent required. Reasonable people
  disagree about exactly where that line sits; know the argument on both sides.
- **Security & acceptable use** — on university systems, you are bound by their policies and
  you own the risk of whatever you expose.
- **Licensing** — know what you're permitted to use as input, and how your own outputs are
  licensed downstream. Think of it the way you'd think about a Creative Commons choice: a
  deliberate decision, not a default.

### Ownership
- **Reproducibility** — could someone else retrace your steps and land in the same place?
- **Accountability** — you own the output, in full, regardless of what tool produced it.

---

## 3. The closing turn

Awareness without paralysis. You are, right now, in a small and well-resourced minority —
not because you earned it more than anyone else, but because a community invested in you.
The ethical resolution isn't guilt about that position, and it isn't denial of the real
costs behind the tools. It's **direction**: use what you've been given to move the benefit
of these tools outward, toward people who don't yet have it, and toward problems worth
solving.

> As long as you're working in the direction of improving humanity's situation, you can be
> comfortable using the energy.

---

## Reflect before you leave

Three questions to sit with, not answer out loud:

1. On the awareness ladder — three Wikipedia articles to *Atlas of AI* — where are you
   honestly standing today?
2. Name one friction point above where you land differently than the person next to you.
   Why?
3. Who is one person you could teach this to in the next month?
